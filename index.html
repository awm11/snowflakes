How the model works

The crystal grows on a hexagonal grid using a cellular automaton based on the Gravner–Griffeath model of snow‑crystal growth. Water vapour diffuses through the chamber and is held at a fixed density far from the crystal, which you set with the humidity control. At the crystal's edge, vapour condenses into a thin boundary layer. A cell freezes onto the crystal once enough mass has built up. Sites with more ice neighbours attach more easily, and that's what makes the facets flat.

Corners and tips stick further out into the vapour, so they collect more of it. At high supersaturation this runs away into branches (the Mullins–Sekerka instability). At low supersaturation, attachment is the slow step, so the facets win and the crystal stays a solid hexagon.

Temperature changes the relative growth rates of the basal facets (top and bottom) and the prism facets (the six sides). Here that's done by tuning the attachment threshold and the in‑plane growth rate against a smoothed version of the measured habit changes. Plates are favoured near −2 °C and −15 °C, and columns near −5 °C and below about −22 °C. The side view is a simpler 1‑D model of thickness along the c‑axis. It isn't a full 3‑D simulation, but it shows the habit changes and records the crystal's history. Thickening slows as the crystal widens, because each new layer on a wide plate needs far more ice. When plates sprout from both ends of a short column, they compete for vapour. Whichever gets slightly ahead shades the other, which leaves a big plate with a stunted partner: a double plate, which Libbrecht notes is common and often mistaken for an ordinary stellar plate. On a longer column the two plates stay similar (a capped column).

Real snowflakes are about 1–5 mm across and take roughly 10–30 minutes to grow as they fall. The time scale here is sped up and the parameter mapping is qualitative.

How the 3D model works

This tab grows the crystal on a 3D lattice of stacked hexagonal layers. It's a version of Gravner and Griffeath's three‑dimensional snow‑crystal model (Phys. Rev. E 79, 011601, 2009). Each cell has six neighbours in its layer and one above and one below. Vapour diffuses through the lattice and is held at a fixed density far from the crystal. Cells freeze onto the crystal once enough mass builds up next to them.

The key ingredient is that the top and bottom (basal) faces and the six side (prism) faces have different attachment thresholds, which depend on temperature. In plate weather, starting a new layer on a basal face is hard, so the crystal spreads sideways into a thin plate. In column weather, side growth is hard, so it grows along the c‑axis. Humidity controls how strongly the crystal is limited by diffusion, which is what makes branches, hollow column ends and needles.

Only one 30° wedge of the upper half is computed, using the crystal's 12‑fold and top/bottom symmetry. The lattice is about 300 cells across, roughly 15 µm per cell for a 4.5 mm flake. The parameter mapping is tuned by hand to give the right shapes in the right parts of the diagram, so it's qualitative, not a calibrated simulation. The top view is an orthographic projection of the same 3D crystal, shaded by its thickness.

How to fold and cut

Tips: thin paper (printer paper or origami paper) works best, because you're cutting through 12 layers. Grey areas are cut away. Either folded edge can be the "arm" edge, because both are mirror lines, so you get the same snowflake either way. Keep at least a little paper along both folds, otherwise the snowflake falls apart.

Based on Nakaya's morphology diagram (1954) and K. G. Libbrecht's work on snow crystal growth. Model after J. Gravner & D. Griffeath, “Modeling snow‑crystal growth: a three‑dimensional mesoscopic approach”, Phys. Rev. E 79 (2009), in its 2‑D hexagonal form.
